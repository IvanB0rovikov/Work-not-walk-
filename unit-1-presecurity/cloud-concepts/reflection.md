   Cloud in your own words
1. Imagine that instead of buying a computer, you rent computing resources over the internet. You pay companies for processors, memory, storage, and networking. Unlike owning a computer, you don’t need to buy or maintain the hardware yourself. You can quickly increase or decrease the resources you use and pay only for what you actually need.

   Traditional → Cloud → Containers
  
2. The evolution of IT is about making systems more flexible, efficient, and easier to manage. Traditional IT relied on physical servers, which could be expensive and underused. Virtualization improved resource usage by running multiple systems on one server. Cloud made resources available on demand without owning hardware. Containers made applications lightweight, portable, and faster to deploy. The overall pattern is greater flexibility and efficiency.

   Deployment vs Service models

3. The main difference is that deployment models describe where resources are located and who manages them, while service models describe what the provider offers and how much responsibility the user has.

Deployment Models
On-Premises — the company buys, hosts, and maintains its own servers and equipment. It provides full control.
Private Cloud — the infrastructure is dedicated to one organization, providing more control and security.
Public Cloud — an external provider supplies the resources. Companies can quickly get computing power without buying their own hardware.
Hybrid Cloud — combines private infrastructure with public resources. It is useful when some data or applications must stay inside the company while others can run externally.
Multi-Cloud — uses several public cloud providers. This reduces dependence on one provider and allows companies to choose different services from different providers.
Service Models
IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) — the provider supplies basic infrastructure such as virtual machines, storage, and networking. You manage the operating system and applications.
PaaS (Platform as a Service) — the provider also manages the platform and environment. You mainly focus on developing and running your application.
SaaS (Software as a Service) — the provider gives you a ready-to-use application. You simply use it through the internet, such as Gmail or Microsoft 365.
In simple terms:
Deployment = where resources are located and how they are managed.
Service = what you receive and how much management work you are responsible for.

   The Shared Responsibility Model 

4. The Shared Responsibility Model means that security is divided between the cloud provider and the customer. Moving to the cloud does not mean the provider is responsible for everything: regardless of whether you use IaaS, PaaS, or SaaS, you are responsible for protecting your data, accounts, passwords, and access permissions. A real example is the 2019 Capital One breach, where a misconfigured firewall and overly broad permissions allowed an attacker to access sensitive data stored in AWS.

   Why organisations still hesitate

5. One reason is regulatory and data residency requirements. Some organisations need to keep certain data within Finland or the EU because of laws and privacy regulations such as GDPR. Another reason is cost predictability. Cloud services are often pay-as-you-go, so costs can increase unexpectedly with higher usage, while on-premise infrastructure can provide more predictable long-term expenses.

   Cloud in an entry-level tech role
  
6. One situation is when a user cannot access their Microsoft 365 account. An entry-level technician may need to check the account, reset the password, or help with MFA. Another situation is receiving an alert about a suspicious sign-in to a cloud account. The technician may verify the user's identity, check the login details, and report or escalate the incident if necessary.

   Personal takeaway

7. I learned a lot about the server-side aspects of IT work—how a server is built, why it’s built, and what challenges might arise during the process—as well as about the cloud and its pros and cons. I also learned the difference between building your own data center and renting someone else’s ready-made cloud server—and it’s not just about the time it takes to build your own center; you need to be forward-thinking so you don’t lose a lot of money waiting for new servers if your startup takes off suddenly and the number of users exceeds what your servers can handle. It was also very interesting to learn about how OnPrem, IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS models work; which is easier to understand if you imagine these models as ways to make a pizza: the OnPrem model is a process where you do all the work yourself—you buy the ingredients, prepare the dough, slice the toppings, and bake the pizza—while, for example, the SaaS model is like going to a restaurant where they do all these steps for you, and they charge you for each service they provide on your behalf.
